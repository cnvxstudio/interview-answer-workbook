# Interview Answer Workbook

A small, reusable workbook for turning a vague interview answer into a specific example. Copy the worksheet, choose a question, and ask a partner to pick a follow-up. You can use everything here offline, without an account or an AI tool.

## Start with one answer

1. Open [the worksheet](WORKSHEET.md) and copy it for your own notes.
2. Choose a prompt from [the question cards](QUESTION-CARDS.md).
3. Write a first answer before reading the follow-ups. Aim for a clear example, rather than a polished script.
4. Answer one follow-up. Add the missing detail to your first answer.
5. Compare the two versions using the checklist below.

Keep your first draft. The comparison is more useful than repeatedly starting from a blank page.

## A practical answer checklist

- **Context:** Can someone outside your team understand the situation?
- **Your responsibility:** Is it clear what you personally owned?
- **Decision:** Did you explain an actual choice and the alternative?
- **Action:** Can a listener picture what you did?
- **Evidence:** Is the result something you observed, or only something you hoped would happen?
- **Reflection:** What would you repeat or change next time?

You do not need an impressive number for every answer. If you did not measure a result, say what you observed and what remains unknown. Never invent metrics, customers, or work experience.

## Example: replacing a vague claim

The following example is fictional. It illustrates answer structure, not a real person's experience.

**Vague:** “I handled competing priorities by communicating with stakeholders.”

**More specific:** “In this fictional project, support wanted an export feature while sales wanted a dashboard. I owned the first recommendation, not the final budget decision. I listed the recurring support requests, compared the work involved, and proposed an export prototype first. The team approved the prototype; its effect on support volume had not yet been measured. Next time I would agree on that measurement before starting.”

**Useful follow-up:** “What evidence would have made you choose the dashboard instead?”

This answer explains the person's part, the decision, and the limits of the evidence. It does not turn an unmeasured outcome into a success claim.

## A simple seven-session plan

Each session can be short. Repeat a session if it reveals a useful gap.

| Session | Practice | Keep in your notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Explain a project to someone outside your field | A clear opening and your responsibility |
| 2 | Describe a difficult choice | The alternative and why you rejected it |
| 3 | Discuss disagreement | What changed after you listened |
| 4 | Describe a mistake | Your response and what remains unresolved |
| 5 | Answer a role-specific question | The assumptions you need to check |
| 6 | Ask a partner for an unexpected follow-up | The detail your first answer omitted |
| 7 | Repeat your first question | A before-and-after comparison |

## Using a practice tool

For a guided text rehearsal, [Practice Mock Interview](https://practicemockinterview.com/) asks a role-specific question, follows up on your answer, and provides written feedback. This workbook is maintained by the same studio; using the website is optional.

Treat feedback as a prompt to revise your answer. It is not a hiring prediction or a substitute for knowing the role. Use anonymized examples and leave out employer secrets, customer details, and other people's personal information.
